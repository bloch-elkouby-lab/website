Shely’s Poster

3RS-SR: Virtual Patient Validation Study

Shelly Khaikin¹, Lauren Lipner², Ronit Shvarzman³, Bernard Gorman⁴, J.C. Muran⁴, Catherine F. Eubanks⁴, Sarah Bloch-Elkouby¹˒³

¹ Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai
² Fairleigh Dickinson University, USA
³ Yeshiva University
⁴ Adelphi University, New York, USA

Background:

The therapeutic alliance has repeatedly been identified as a robust predictor of psychotherapy outcomes and retention (Flückiger et al., 2018). Alliance ruptures, or breakdowns in the collaboration or agreement of psychotherapy tasks and goals or in the affective bond, are common in psychotherapy and predict poorer outcomes when unrepaired (Eubanks et al., 2018).

Although the Rupture Resolution Rating Scale (3RS) is a well-validated observer-based measure used to identify patient rupture markers and therapist repair strategies within psychotherapy sessions (Eubanks et al., 2015), the self-report version (3RS-SR) has yet to be validated in the general population, let alone with a high-risk patient population.

Study Aims:

The aim of this study was to take advantage of clinicians’ interactions with a standardized virtual patient at high risk for suicide to assess the psychometric properties of the 3RS-SR instrument.

Method:

Seventy psychologists completed the 3RS-SR based on their clinical interactions with a virtual adolescent patient. The study assessed the factor structure, internal consistency, interrater reliability, and convergent validity of the measure.

Analytic approach:

  • Principal component analysis to assess factor structure.
  • Cronbach’s alpha to assess internal consistency.
  • McDonald’s omega to assess reliability.
  • (r_{wg}) to assess interrater reliability.
  • Pearson’s (r) to assess convergent validity.
Study characteristic Description
Sample size 70 psychologists
Study design Virtual patient validation study
Clinical context Clinician interactions with a virtual adolescent patient at high risk for suicide
Measure Rupture Resolution Rating Scale–Self Report (3RS-SR)
Psychometric properties assessed Factor structure, internal consistency, interrater reliability, and convergent validity
Statistical analyses Principal component analysis, Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, (r_{wg}), and Pearson’s (r)

Results:

The measure demonstrated good fit for a three-factor solution. Overall internal consistency across all items was moderate, with good reliability for the Working Together and Rupture subscales. Interrater reliability was moderate, and convergent validity was good.

Psychometric property Statistic Interpretation
Factor structure 3-factor solution Good model fit
Overall internal consistency Cronbach’s α = .56 Moderate reliability
Working Together subscale Cronbach’s α = .83 Good reliability
Rupture subscale Cronbach’s α = .73 Acceptable–good reliability
Interrater reliability – Working Together subscale (r_{wg}) = -0.4037 Adequate agreement between raters
Interrater reliability – Rupture subscale (r_{wg}) = -0.74745 Adequate agreement between raters
Convergent validity – Working Together subscale and Therapist Response Questionnaire (TRQ) disaffiliation Pearson’s (r) = -0.726 Supports construct validity

Discussion:

The study’s findings suggest that the 3RS-SR is a psychometrically valid and reliable diagnostic tool to assess the presence of alliance and rupture processes with virtual patients at high risk for suicide.

Findings support the factor structure of the measure and demonstrate acceptable interrater reliability and convergent validity. Interrater reliability is consistent with the inherently subjective nature of alliance rupture processes, suggesting that the 3RS-SR captures meaningful variability in clinicians’ responses.

Future studies should replicate these results with actual patient samples.

References:

Eubanks, C. F., Muran, J. C., & Safran, J. D. (2015). Rupture resolution rating system (3RS): Manual. Mount Sinai-Beth Israel Medical Center, New York.

Eubanks, C. F., Muran, J. C., & Safran, J. D. (2018). Alliance rupture repair: A meta-analysis. Psychotherapy, 55(4), 508–519.
