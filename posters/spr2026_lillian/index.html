Lilian's Poster

Countertransference and the Accuracy of Suicide-Risk Assessment in Virtual Patient Clinical Interviews

Lilian Guo¹˒²˒³, Jimmy P. Chen¹˒², & Sarah Bloch-Elkouby¹˒²

¹ Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, New York, NY, USA
² Ferkauf Graduate School of Psychology, Yeshiva University, New York, NY, USA
³ Teachers College, Columbia University, New York, NY, USA

Background:

Countertransference refers to clinicians’ emotional, cognitive, and behavioral reactions toward patients (Gabbard, 2001). Suicidal patients often evoke strong emotional responses in clinicians, and such reactions may shape clinical judgment and intervention processes (Barzilay et al., 2018; Michaud et al., 2021). However, little is known about whether countertransference is associated with clinicians’ accuracy in identifying suicide risk and Suicide Crisis Syndrome (SCS) features during standardized virtual patient interviews.

Virtual patients (VPs) offer a scalable way to standardize suicide-risk assessment scenarios while holding patient presentation constant across clinicians.

Study Aims:

  1. Examine whether countertransference is associated with clinicians’ accuracy in suicide-risk assessment.
  2. Examine whether patient race influences assessment accuracy or moderates the relationship between countertransference and assessment accuracy.
  3. Exploratorily examine whether clinicians’ perceptions of SCS severity are associated with countertransference reactions.

Method:

This analysis used data from a larger virtual patient study in which 154 clinicians completed an intake interview with a standardized virtual patient, “Noah.” Participants were assigned to one of two race conditions: Black Noah (n = 84; Group 1) or White Noah (n = 70; Group 2). Noah responded through pre-scripted text and video responses designed to simulate a suicide-risk intake interview.

Following the interview, clinicians completed a survey that included questions for the following measures:

  1. Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS; Posner et al., 2011).
  2. Suicide Crisis Syndrome Checklist (SCS-C; Bloch-Elkouby et al., 2026).
  3. Therapist Response Questionnaire–Suicide Form (TRQ-SF; Barzilay et al., 2018), assessing:
    • Distress
    • Disaffiliation
    • Hopelessness

Accuracy Scoring

Assessment inaccuracy was calculated by comparing clinician ratings with expert-derived benchmark ratings based on Noah’s scripted dialogue. C-SSRS ratings were converted to highest endorsed risk level, with levels 3–4 treated as benchmark-concordant. SCS-C Likert ratings were dichotomized into symptom present/absent scores, and total SCS-C inaccuracy reflected the proportion of items inconsistent with benchmark ratings.

Statistical Approach

Pearson correlations, Welch’s t-tests, and linear regression models examined associations among countertransference, assessment inaccuracy, and patient race.

Results:

1. Associations Between Countertransference and Assessment Inaccuracy

No significant correlations were found between countertransference and clinicians’ accuracy in identifying suicide risk or Suicide Crisis Syndrome features.

C-SSRS Inaccuracy TRQ Total -0.03 0.697
C-SSRS Inaccuracy Distress 0.05 0.512
C-SSRS Inaccuracy Disaffiliation -0.04 0.660
C-SSRS Inaccuracy Hopelessness -0.10 0.232
SCS-C Inaccuracy TRQ Total -0.03 0.699
SCS-C Inaccuracy Distress -0.02 0.827
SCS-C Inaccuracy Disaffiliation 0.02 0.814
SCS-C Inaccuracy Hopelessness -0.14 0.089

Negative correlations indicate that higher countertransference scores were associated with lower assessment inaccuracy, reflecting slightly greater accuracy.

Exploratory analyses revealed a small negative association between Hopelessness and SCS-C inaccuracy (r = -0.14, p = .089), although this finding did not reach statistical significance.

2. Race Effects

No significant differences in assessment inaccuracy were observed across patient race conditions. Patient race also did not significantly moderate the association between countertransference and assessment inaccuracy.

3. Perceived SCS Severity and Countertransference

Greater perceived SCS severity was marginally associated with lower Disaffiliation (r = -0.15, p = .066). This suggests a possible trend in which clinicians who perceived Noah as exhibiting more severe Suicide Crisis Syndrome symptoms reported less disaffiliative countertransference.

Discussion:

These findings suggest that, within this virtual patient paradigm, clinicians maintained suicide-risk assessment accuracy despite experiencing emotional reactions toward the patient. Exploratory findings further suggest that countertransference may operate through more nuanced mechanisms than direct effects on accuracy.

Specifically, greater hopelessness was associated with lower SCS-C inaccuracy, and greater perceived SCS severity was associated with lower Disaffiliation, indicating that clinicians’ emotional reactions may shape engagement with and interpretation of suicide-related distress even when overall assessment accuracy is unaffected.

Noah was a research-team-designed virtual patient using scripted responses; therefore, findings may not fully generalize to real clinical interactions. Pre-scripted or nonsensical virtual patient responses may disrupt conversational flow and reduce ecological validity. Future work should examine countertransference and suicide-risk assessment using more ecologically valid virtual patients developed from real patient narratives or clinical transcripts.
