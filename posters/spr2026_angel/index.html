Exploring Clinicians’ Experience of Therapeutic Alliance Ruptures and Repairs with High-Risk Patients: A Proposed Study

Yongxue (Angel) Wei¹˒³, Jingyi (Violet) Yang¹, Madison Wakely¹, & Sarah Bloch-Elkouby²˒³

1 Teachers College, Columbia University in the City of New York
2 Ferkauf Graduate School of Psychology, Yeshiva University
3 Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City

Background:

Suicide claims over 727,000 lives annually worldwide (WHO, 2025). Clinicians play a crucial role in suicide-risk assessment and intervention, yet establishing and maintaining a therapeutic alliance with high-risk patients presents unique challenges.

Despite the therapeutic alliance’s well-established contribution to treatment outcomes, research on rupture-repair processes with suicidal patients, as well as clinician characteristics that may facilitate or hinder these processes, remains limited.

Notably, approximately two-thirds of individuals who died by suicide did not report suicidal ideation at their last clinical visit within 30 days prior to death (Berman, 2018; Busch et al., 2003), highlighting the importance of understanding clinicians’ experiences and therapeutic relationships when working with high-risk patients.

The current study examines how clinicians’ emotion regulation difficulties, perceived competence, and self-compassion relate to the quality of the therapeutic alliance and rupture-repair processes in high-risk clinical contexts.

Hypothesis:

H1: Clinicians’ emotion regulation difficulties will be negatively associated with therapeutic alliance quality.

H2: Clinicians’ perceived competence and comfort will be positively associated with therapeutic alliance quality.

H3: Clinicians’ self-compassion will be positively associated with rupture-repair effectiveness.</span>

Method:

The current study employs a cross-sectional survey design targeting 175 clinicians and clinicians-in-training who conduct individual therapy with patients experiencing active suicidal ideation. The study aims to achieve 90% statistical power to detect correlations of r ≥ 0.30, with a significance level of α = .05.

Eligible licensed participants include psychologists, psychiatrists, social workers, licensed professional counselors (LPCs), marriage and family therapists (MFTs), and other licensed mental health professionals. Participants in training include graduate students pursuing master’s or doctoral degrees in clinical or counseling psychology, mental health counseling, marriage and family therapy, social work, or related disciplines. Pre-licensed participants include psychology interns, postdoctoral fellows, psychiatry residents, clinical social work interns, and associate or provisional counselors.

Clinician characteristics will be assessed through five self-report measures: a. Suicide Prevention Clinician Self-Efficacy Scale (SPCS) (Jahn et al., 2016), measuring clinician comfort; b. Suicide Competency Assessment Form (SCAF) (Cramer et al., 2013), measuring perceived competency; c. Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale–Short Form (DERS-SF) (Kaufman et al., 2016), measuring emotion regulation difficulties; d. Self-Compassion Scale–Short Form (SCS-SF) (Raes et al., 2011), measuring self-compassion; and e. Rupture Resolution Rating System–Self Report (3RS-SR) (Bloch-Elkouby et al., 2026), measuring therapeutic alliance and rupture-repair processes.

An additional measure, the Alliance with High-Risk Patients Inventory (AHRPI), developed for the current study, will assess clinicians’ experiences of therapeutic alliance with high-risk patients.

Data will be collected through an IRB-approved Qualtrics survey. Following recruitment through professional networks, social media, and institutional lists, participants will complete informed consent, eligibility screening, demographic questions, and self-report measures. Data quality will be assessed through CAPTCHA, bot detection, and attention checks. Descriptive, correlational, and hierarchical regression analyses will examine associations between clinician characteristics, therapeutic alliance quality, and rupture-repair processes. The study will also evaluate the psychometric properties of the newly developed AHRPI.

Discussion:

The proposed study aims to clarify how clinician characteristics, including emotion regulation difficulties, perceived competence, comfort, and self-compassion, relate to therapeutic alliance quality and rupture-repair processes when working with high-risk patients.

Emotion regulation difficulties are expected to negatively relate to alliance quality, whereas clinician competence and comfort are expected to positively relate to alliance quality. Additionally, self-compassion is anticipated to support effective rupture repair, with withdrawal ruptures, characterized by patient concealment of suicidal ideation, expected to be the most frequently endorsed rupture type.

The development and psychometric evaluation of the AHRPI will provide an opportunity to establish a measure specifically designed to assess therapeutic alliance and rupture-repair dynamics in suicide-risk contexts. An interpretable factor structure and adequate internal consistency are anticipated.

Clinically, findings may clarify whether clinician competence, emotion regulation, and self-compassion should be targeted in training to strengthen therapeutic alliance, informing both clinical education and future research on alliance dynamics in suicide-risk contexts.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences between clinician characteristics and alliance outcomes. Second, reliance on self-report measures introduces potential social desirability and retrospective recall biases.

Future research should employ longitudinal and session-level designs to capture the dynamic nature of alliance ruptures and repairs over the course of treatment. Randomized controlled trials could also evaluate targeted training interventions aimed at enhancing clinicians’ capacity to maintain and repair the therapeutic alliance with high-risk patients.
