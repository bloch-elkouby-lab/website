<h2 id="acceptability-appropriateness-and-feasibility-of-a-training-module-targeting-clinicians-negative-emotional-responses-to-suicidal-patients-a-pilot">Acceptability, Appropriateness, and Feasibility of a Training Module Targeting Clinicians’ Negative Emotional Responses to Suicidal Patients: A Pilot</h2>

<p>by <strong>You-Syuan Lin</strong>¹˒²˒³, <strong>Kate Van Houten</strong>¹˒²˒³, <strong>Samuel Welsh</strong>¹˒²˒³, <strong>Hanjiang Xu</strong>¹˒²˒³, &amp; <strong>Sarah Bloch-Elkouby</strong>¹˒²</p>

<p>¹ Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai<br />
² Ferkauf Graduate School of Psychology, Yeshiva University<br />
³ Teachers College, Columbia University</p>

<div style="width: 100%; margin: 20px 0;">
  <iframe src="/website/research/naspr_2026/Angela_NASPR.pdf" width="100%" height="900px" style="border: 1px solid #ddd;" title="Angel's NASPR 2026 Poster">
  </iframe>
</div>

<h3 id="background">Background</h3>

<p>Clinicians’ negative emotional responses may nonverbally convey discomfort, frustration, judgment, or withdrawal, especially when working with high-risk patients.</p>

<p>Suicidal patients may be especially sensitive to perceived rejection or invalidation. Therefore, recognizing and managing these responses is critical to improving empathic communication and therapeutic alliance.</p>

<p>Our lab developed a training module providing brief psychoeducation on clinicians’ negative emotional responses to suicidal patients and evidence-based skills for managing them during clinical interactions.</p>

<h3 id="research-questions">Research Questions</h3>

<ol>
  <li>Do participants find the Emotional Response Training Module appropriate, feasible, and acceptable to incorporate in their training working with high-risk suicidal patients?</li>
  <li>Do participants find the Emotional Response Training Module content and structure satisfying, or is there further feedback on the delivery of the content?</li>
</ol>

<h3 id="methods">Methods</h3>

<p><strong>Sample:</strong> N = 10 graduate students, ages 21–38 (M = 26.30, SD = 4.97). Eight participants had direct clinical interaction experience; four identified as White, one as Black, five as Asian, and six were female.</p>

<p><strong>Procedure:</strong> After providing consent, participants completed a demographic survey, the training module, and post-module implementation measures via Qualtrics.</p>

<p><strong>Measures:</strong> Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility were assessed using the Acceptability of Intervention Measure, Intervention Appropriateness Measure, and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (AIM/IAM/FIM; Weiner et al., 2017).</p>

<p>Participants were also encouraged to provide additional suggestions and thoughts about the module in a text box to help improve the module content or delivery method.</p>

<h3 id="results">Results</h3>

<p>Textbox feedback was positive in supporting the module’s perceived relevance, particularly its focus on an understudied aspect of clinicians’ experiences when working with suicidal patients.</p>

<p>Participants highlighted the usefulness of the training video and its focus on clinician emotional regulation.</p>

<p>Suggestions for improvement included:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Addressing clinician stress and pressure during high-risk encounters.</li>
  <li>Providing greater context to normalize negative emotional responses.</li>
  <li>Incorporating additional opportunities for supervision or follow-up.</li>
  <li>Allowing more time to discuss the measures presented in the training video.</li>
</ul>

<h3 id="discussion">Discussion</h3>

<p>These findings provide support for the module as a potentially useful training resource and identify specific areas for refinement prior to further evaluation.</p>

<p>Participant feedback supported the relevance of addressing clinicians’ negative emotional responses, an understudied aspect of working with suicidal patients.</p>

<p>Participants also emphasized the importance of providing context for these responses and offering additional support for clinicians following challenging encounters.</p>

<h3 id="limitations">Limitations</h3>

<p>The sample was very small and consisted of psychology graduate students, limiting generalizability to practicing clinicians and other professionals.</p>

<p>Results were based on self-reported perceptions immediately following the module and therefore do not establish whether training changes clinicians’ emotional responses or clinical behavior.</p>

<h3 id="future-directions">Future Directions</h3>

<p>Discuss with the broader team whether clinician stress and pressure during high-risk encounters, including strategies for managing these responses in the moment, should be included.</p>

<p>Consider adding introductory context to normalize and destigmatize negative emotional responses and clarify how these responses may arise during suicide-focused clinical encounters.</p>

<p>Consider incorporating supervisor follow-up or reflective supervision to support clinicians’ emotional processing after challenging encounters.</p>

Psychometric assessment of three newly developed implementation outcome measures. <em>Implementation Science, 12</em>(1), 108. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13012-017-0635-3</p>
