Sam's Poster

Acceptability, Appropriateness, and Feasibility of a Training Module Targeting Clinician’s Ability to Respond to Empathic Opportunities in Suicidal Patients - Pilot Study

Samuel Welsh¹˒²˒³*, You-Syuan Lin¹˒²˒³, & Sarah Bloch-Elkouby¹˒²

¹ Ferkauf Graduate School of Psychology, Yeshiva University
² Mount Sinai Behavioral Health Center
³ Teachers College, Columbia University

Background

Empathy has long been hypothesized as a key element of the change process in psychotherapy. It can be clinically defined as understanding what another person is experiencing, or trying to express. Empirical literature has indicated the positive association of empathy with treatment outcomes in numerous clinical settings. While empathy is understood to be a core component of a healthy therapeutic alliance, the optimal ways to increase it require further exploration, especially in regard to clinical encounters with high-risk patients.

The goal of this study is to develop and assess the appropriateness, feasibility, and acceptability of a new training module based on the Empathic Communication Coding System framework, a validated observer-based measure of clinicians’ responses to empathic opportunities in therapeutic interactions. This module provides a didactic training for clinicians to help them develop effective verbal empathy skills when interacting with patients in general, and high-risk patients in particular.

Research Questions and Hypotheses

1. Do participants find the Verbal Empathy Training Module appropriate, feasible, and acceptable to incorporate in their training working with patients, especially those who are considered high-risk?

  • H1: We hypothesize high perceived acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility, indicating that the module is understandable, clinically relevant, and practical for training clinicians to increase their usage of verbal empathy during patient interactions.

2. Do participants find the Verbal Empathy Training Module an effective way to teach clinicians how to increase their verbal empathy?

  • H2: We hypothesize that participants will exhibit high perceived effectiveness for this training, and regard it as a suitable way of increasing verbal empathy in clinicians.

Method

  • N = 15 individuals with a graduate degree or higher in psychology or a related field participated in the pilot.
  • Participants completed the demographic survey, the module, and post-module implementation and perceived effectiveness measures in Qualtrics.
  • Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility were assessed using modified versions of the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM).
  • Perceived effectiveness of training was evaluated using the Training Evaluation Inventory (TEI).
  • Participants were encouraged to offer their opinions of the module and suggestions for its improvement in open text boxes.

Results

The training module was perceived favorably across all four measured areas:

Measure Mean
Acceptability 3.833
Appropriateness 4.117
Feasibility 3.983
Perceived effectiveness 4.076

All scores were above the neutral midpoint of 3.

Participant Feedback

Feedback was generally positive, with special praise given to the module’s visuals, the information provided on empathy, and the level of engagement achieved.

Participants highlighted that:

  • The module appropriately highlighted the importance of empathy.
  • General guidelines are not the way to go; patients should be responded to on a case-by-case basis.
  • The module may be helpful for clinicians for whom this style of interacting with patients is not common.
  • The visuals were calming and relevant to the material being delivered.
  • The levels of the ECCS module were explained well and went beyond simply highlighting the importance of empathy.
  • The module did a good job of showing that empathy has discrete “borders.”
  • The module was engaging and easy to follow.

Suggestions for Improvement

Participants suggested:

  • The content would be better received if delivered by real people with a demonstration.
  • The module should discuss how different patients may require clinicians to employ empathy in different ways.
  • The length should be shortened.
  • A summary slide at the end covering the different ECCS levels would be useful.
  • Greater amounts of time should be devoted to ECCS levels 0–2.
  • Examples should follow a single patient with the same issue rather than showcasing a range of different issues.
  • More time should be devoted to explaining how using empathy in a session will benefit a patient over the long run.
  • More examples would help participants better understand the material.
  • The module should address how patients may react to realizing that clinicians are responding to empathic opportunities in predetermined ways, and discuss other approaches clinicians can rely on when demonstrating empathy.

Discussion

The data indicate that the training module was perceived favorably across acceptability (M = 3.833), appropriateness (M = 4.117), feasibility (M = 3.983), and perceived effectiveness (M = 4.076), with all scores above the neutral midpoint of 3.

Limitations and Future Directions

The sample size (N = 15) was limited, as this was only a pilot study. Participants were majority female (73%), and 73% possessed a master’s degree as their highest level of education.

Future studies should first and foremost attempt to have a much larger and more diverse sample. The module should also be changed to accommodate the provided feedback, particularly through greater emphasis on how patients respond when clinicians demonstrate higher levels of empathy and the inclusion of more concrete examples.
