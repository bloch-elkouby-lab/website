## Introduction

Despite increased awareness of racial disparities in healthcare, implicit racial biases continue to persist and influence clinical practice. Although explicit racial biases have declined, implicit biases have decreased at a slower rate, suggesting that unconscious prejudices remain prevalent.

Implicit racial biases have been shown to negatively impact clinical decisions, weaken therapeutic alliances, and contribute to poorer treatment outcomes for racially minoritized patients. However, few studies have examined how implicit racial biases influence professionals' interactions with Black adolescents, a group disproportionately affected by suicide risk and systemic inequities in mental healthcare.

## Objective

This study uses Virtual Human Interaction (VHI) technology to simulate realistic clinical encounters and examine how implicit racial biases influence clinicians' engagement and enjoyment in interactions with racially diverse adolescent patients.

Specifically, the study examined:

1. Whether virtual patients' race and clinicians' implicit racial biases impact clinicians' interaction time.
2. Whether virtual patients' race and clinicians' implicit racial biases impact clinicians' enjoyment of clinical interviews.
3. Whether these relationships are moderated by clinicians' own racial identity.

## Hypotheses

1. Clinicians with higher levels of implicit racial bias would be associated with shorter clinical interviews and lower enjoyment.
2. Clinicians would conduct shorter interviews and report lower enjoyment with Black virtual patients compared to White virtual patients.
3. The moderating effect of clinicians' race was not hypothesized due to the lack of prior research.

## Method

**Participants:** 138 licensed and pre-licensed mental health professionals recruited from clinical psychology programs across the United States (M = 28.74, SD = 3.57, range = 22–48 years).

**Design:** Participants were randomly assigned to interact with a virtual patient of either race (Black or White).

**Procedure:** Clinicians participated in simulated clinical interactions with virtual patients using Virtual Human Interaction (VHI) technology.

**Measures:**

1. **Implicit racial bias:** Race Implicit Association Test (IAT), categorized as high White preference, neutral, or high Black preference.
2. **Interaction length:** Time spent interacting with the virtual patient, measured in minutes by the VHI system.
3. **Enjoyment:** Self-reported enjoyment of the clinical interview using a 1-item, 5-point Likert scale (1 = Poor, 5 = Excellent).
4. **Clinician race:** Self-reported racial identity, categorized as White, Black, and Other.

**Statistical Analysis:** Separate linear regression models examined interaction length and enjoyment. Models tested the effects of virtual patients' race and clinicians' implicit racial biases, with clinician race as a moderator. Analyses controlled for clinician age, biological sex, and sexual orientation. Bootstrap resampling with replacement was used to balance clinician racial groups, and sensitivity analyses were conducted using the original unbalanced dataset.

## Results

### 1. Interaction Length

There was no indication that the race of the virtual patient alone affected interaction length.

Neutral implicit racial bias predicted longer sessions overall compared to high White or Black preference. However, among White clinicians, neutral bias predicted shorter sessions overall.

White clinicians spent significantly more time with virtual patients than Black clinicians, regardless of virtual patient race.

### 2. Enjoyment

Clinicians reported greater enjoyment when interacting with White virtual patients. However, this relationship was moderated by clinician race, with non-White clinicians reporting lower enjoyment during interactions with White virtual patients.

Among White clinicians, neutral implicit bias was associated with higher enjoyment compared to Black preference bias.

### 3. Sensitivity Analysis

When the same regression models were conducted using the original, unbalanced dataset, key effects were no longer statistically significant. This likely reflects limited statistical power due to small sample sizes among underrepresented clinician racial groups.

## Discussion

The findings provide exploratory evidence that clinicians' implicit racial biases and their own racial identities may influence the length and enjoyment of clinical interactions with virtual patients.

The study highlights several considerations:

1. The sample was skewed toward young, female, and White clinicians, limiting generalizability.
2. Future studies should include more Black and male clinicians across a wider age range to improve group balance and statistical power.
3. Additional factors, including technical issues, therapeutic alliance measures, and clinicians' training, may influence study outcomes.

This study provides valuable exploratory evidence about how clinicians' implicit racial biases and their own racial identities can influence clinical interactions, highlighting factors that may be critical components of effective mental healthcare.
