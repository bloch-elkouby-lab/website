### Background: ###

Suicide is a major public health concern, and mental health clinicians play a vital role in suicide risk assessment and intervention. Consistently working with patients at high risk for suicide can negatively affect clinicians’ well-being. Self-compassion may help reduce emotional stress and promote approach-oriented coping among clinicians (Galili-Weinstock et al., 2020; Keyes et al., 2023).

Clinicians frequently report feeling inadequately prepared to work with high-risk patients, and additional education may improve self-reported competency (Cramer et al., 2013). The therapeutic alliance is also an essential component of psychotherapy, particularly when discussing suicidal ideation. A weak alliance may contribute to trust issues and client resistance (Zarzycka et al., 2022).

Although prior research has examined clinician self-compassion and the therapeutic alliance, limited research has investigated whether self-compassion is associated with alliance ruptures specifically among clinicians working with high-risk patients. The relationships between self-compassion, perceived comfort, and competency in this clinical population also remain underexplored.

### Study Aims: ###

This study aims to explore the relationship between clinician self-compassion, therapeutic alliance ruptures, and clinicians’ self-reported competency and comfort when working with patients at high risk for suicide.

The study will address two primary research areas:

1. **Comfort and self-reported competency:** Examine whether clinician self-compassion is associated with perceived comfort and competency when working with high-risk patients, and whether years of experience are related to self-reported comfort and competency.
2. **Therapeutic alliance and ruptures:** Examine the relationship between clinician self-compassion and the quality of therapeutic alliance ruptures with high-risk patients.

Given the limited literature on these relationships, the research questions are exploratory and no hypotheses will be formulated.

### Method: ###

This study will use a survey-based design to examine clinicians’ self-reported experiences working with patients at high risk for suicide.

**Participants:** Approximately 85–100 mental health clinicians across diverse practice settings, including psychologists, psychiatrists, social workers, and counselors.

**Inclusion criteria:**
- Current licensure as a mental health clinician.
- Currently working with, or having previously worked with, patients presenting with active suicidal ideation.
- Minimum age of 18 years.
- Ability to understand and complete English-language questionnaires.
- Ability to understand and provide electronic informed consent.

**Procedure:** Participants will access the study through secure Qualtrics links, review study information, and provide informed consent. The survey is expected to take approximately 30–35 minutes to complete.

**Measures:**
- Self-Compassion Scale–Short Form (SCS-SF).
- Rupture Resolution Rating System, Therapist Version (3RS-SF); adapted.
- Suicide Competency Inventory (SCI).
- Suicide Knowledge and Skills Questionnaire (SKSQ); adapted.
- Demographic questions.

| Study characteristic | Description |
|---|---|
| Study design | Survey-based, exploratory study |
| Target sample | Approximately 85–100 mental health clinicians |
| Population | Clinicians working with or previously working with patients presenting with active suicidal ideation |
| Recruitment | Secure Qualtrics survey links |
| Survey duration | Approximately 30–35 minutes |
| Primary variables | Self-compassion, therapeutic alliance ruptures, perceived comfort, and self-reported competency |
| Additional variable | Years of clinical experience |

### Proposed Analyses: ###

Data will be analyzed using SPSS.

| Analysis | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Cronbach’s alpha | Assess internal reliability of the full scale and subscales |
| Pearson correlation | Examine the association between clinician self-compassion and comfort and competency, as well as the relationship between years of experience and self-reported comfort and competency |
| Point-biserial correlation | Assess the relationship between clinician self-compassion and therapeutic alliance ruptures |

### Discussion: ###

Consistently working with patients at high risk for suicide can create significant professional pressure and emotional strain for clinicians. Understanding factors that support clinicians’ well-being and clinical functioning may help improve patient care and clinical outcomes.

This study may inform clinician training by emphasizing the importance of self-compassion when working with high-risk patients. Examining the relationships between self-compassion, perceived comfort, competency, and therapeutic alliance ruptures may also help identify areas where clinicians need additional education and support.
